Research

We study the organisms that shape forests and green spaces, and the interactions among them. By combining field observations and experiments, molecular ecological analyses, statistical modelling and simulation, we link information obtained from different taxa and different levels of organization (plants, microbes, populations, communities and landscapes) to understand how ecosystems work and how they change over time.

Rather than treating individual phenomena as separate problems, we aim to understand them as connected parts of forests and green spaces as complex systems.

Research Themes

Forest dynamics: measurement and modelling

How do trees grow, die and replace one another? We measure forest structure and its change using tree censuses, LiDAR and remote sensing, and quantify growth, mortality and regeneration with hierarchical Bayesian models and functional–structural tree models. Our goal is to predict how forests respond to environmental change and disturbance.

  • Selected papers
  • Iida et al. (2023) Journal of Ecology
  • Umeki et al. (2018) Forests
  • Fukuchi et al. (2020) Papers on Environmental Information Science (in Japanese)

Diversity of soil microbial communities

Forest soils harbour an enormous diversity of bacteria and fungi. Using DNA sequencing, we examine how microbial communities change with elevation, season, topography and vegetation, and what determines their diversity. We are also developing Bayesian adaptive sampling to capture whole communities efficiently from a limited number of samples.

  • Selected papers
  • Shigyo et al. (2022) CATENA
  • Shigyo et al. (2019) Frontiers in Microbiology
  • Shigyo et al. (2019) FEMS Microbiology Ecology

Litter decomposition and nutrient cycling

We study how leaf litter is decomposed and how carbon and nitrogen are recycled, with a focus on the role of microbes. Topics include the home-field advantage, in which litter decomposes faster in its home habitat, and the role of free-living nitrogen fixers in litter and soil, studied with field manipulation experiments and molecular approaches.

  • Selected papers
  • Shigyo et al. (2024) New Phytologist
  • Hirao et al. (2021) Pedobiologia

Deer and forest ecosystems

Overabundant sika deer affect not only understory vegetation but also the insects, birds and soils that depend on it. Through continued surveys and deer-exclosure experiments in the Okuchichibu Mountains, central Japan, we examine how these effects spread through the ecosystem and how vegetation recovers.

  • Selected papers
  • Tanigawa et al. (2022) Mammalian Biology
  • Makino et al. (2020) Papers on Environmental Information Science (in Japanese)
